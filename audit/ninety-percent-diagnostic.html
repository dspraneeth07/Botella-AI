Engine Three · diagnostic · the route to a 90% guarantee

One number is doing two jobs

The engine tells the owner to expect the average outcome. An average is beaten about half the time by construction — so the promise fails roughly half the time no matter how good the forecast gets. Ninety percent is reachable, and the first step is arithmetic, not a model.

Date 5 October 2026 Scope every component of the live engine, read in source Baseline 75.9% selection · promise kept at 1 of 5 stores · 4 of 5 net negative

What is actually wrong, in three sentences

One. The quantity on the card is the mean of the demand gap. For a product selling 14 units a month, the probability that reality reaches its own mean is 53.5% — so a promise built on the mean is a coin flip even with a perfect forecast. Promise 10 units instead of 14 and the same forecast keeps its word 89.1% of the time. That single change is most of the way to 90%, and it needs no new data and no new model.

Two. The engine uses that same mean to decide how much to buy, and there it is too small. For a bottle carrying $10 of margin and about $1 a cycle of carrying cost, the economically correct stocking point is the 91st percentile — 19 units, not 14. Under-stocking costs margin; over-stocking liquor costs pennies, because it does not spoil. The order quantity and the promise are two different numbers and the engine prints one.

Three. The confidence figure on every card is not calibrated. It is p_hit, an algebraic ratio, passed through a calibration stage whose fitted curve does not exist because recommendation_evaluations has zero rows — so it falls through unchanged. "74% likely" is currently an assertion, not a frequency. No accuracy guarantee of any kind can be made until that number is earned.

Beyond those three, the selection ceiling of roughly 73% is an information limit, not a modelling one: at the sizes this engine picks, a 27% dip is one standard deviation of pure chance, so telling a noise dip from a real decline is not possible from counts alone. Breaking that ceiling needs new inputs — on-hand, receipts, price events — and section 06 says which to start capturing tonight.

Section 01

The one-number mistake

This is the finding that matters most, so it goes first. Take a product selling about 14 units in 30 days — the median pick the engine actually makes. Demand over a window is a count, so its distribution is roughly Poisson, and that distribution is not symmetric around a useful decision point.

Why this is the highest-leverage change in the whole engine

It explains both open problems at once. The promise is unkept because the mean is promised; the stores are net negative because the mean is also ordered, and the mean is below the economically correct stocking point for a high-margin, non-perishable good — so the engine simultaneously over-promises and under-stocks, then gets charged for the stock it did buy.

And it is cheap. app/pipeline/policy/inventory.py already computes a service-level quantity from the p10–p90 interval and already refuses to answer without one. The interval already exists and is already calibrated to 80–82% coverage. The work is routing the live path through it and splitting one field into two.

The arithmetic, so it can be checked

A product whose true rate is 14 units per 30 days. Demand is a COUNT, so the
spread is sqrt(14) = 3.74 units — about 27% of the mean. Nothing removes it.

  promise 14  (the mean — what the card says today)   kept 53.5% of the time
  promise 12                                         kept 74.0%
  promise 11  (p25)                                  kept 82.4%
  promise 10                                         kept 89.1%   ← the target
  promise  9  (p10)                                  kept 93.8%

And the ORDER quantity runs the other way, set by the cost asymmetry:

     q* = Cu / (Cu + Co)        Cu = margin lost if it stocks out
                                Co = cost of holding one extra unit a cycle

  $10 margin, $0.50 carrying  ->  q* = 95.2%   order to the p95
  $10 margin, $1.00 carrying  ->  q* = 90.9%   order to the p91 = 19 units
  $10 margin, $4.00 carrying  ->  q* = 71.4%   order to the p71

Liquor does not spoil, so Co is small and q* is HIGH. The current service
levels (policy.service_level.*) are PROVISIONAL business guesses. They should
be DERIVED from this SKU's own margin and carrying cost, per SKU, per cycle.
And a measurement error that makes the profit picture look worse than it is

The ledger charges over-ordering at over_units × full unit cost (daily_recommendation_ledger.py:203). That treats every unsold bottle as a total write-off. Liquor does not spoil. An unsold bottle is inventory that sells next month; its true cost is capital tied up plus shelf opportunity — cents on the dollar per cycle, not the whole cost of goods.

So the headline "four of five stores are net negative" is partly an artifact of charging 100% of COGS against a card whose stock is still on the shelf and still saleable. This must be corrected before anyone concludes the engine loses money, and it must be corrected before the sizing change, or the sizing change will be measured against a broken ruler.

Section 02

Why 90% cannot come from the current metric

The number being quoted as accuracy today is the hit rate: did a recommended product beat its own trailing rate. It is at 75.9%. Pushing it to 90% is not an engineering target — it is arithmetically out of reach, and the engine's own settings file already says so with measurements.

Four things "90% accurate" could mean, and which are reachable

This is the decision to make, because the engineering follows from it. Only one of these is being measured today, and it is the one that cannot get there.

The recommendation

Stop quoting D1 as the accuracy number. Keep measuring it — it is the right way to test whether selection is improving, and the +16.6 point edge over a reversion-matched null is a real result. But it is a research metric, not a promise to an owner, and it has a lid.

Make D2 and D3 the guarantees on the card, both at 90%, both verifiable by the owner from their own till. Then make D4 the number the business is run on. A store owner has never once asked whether a product beat its trailing rate.

The six that matter, in full

F1 · F2

One field is being asked to be both a floor and a ceiling

critical · arithmetic

deficit_units is the expected gap between the long-run rate and the recent rate over the horizon. It is an expectation. It is then used, unchanged, as the number the card promises and the number the owner is told to order.

Evidence

app/recommender/deterministic.py: qty = max(1, int(round(p.deficit_units))) — and the same figure is formatted into the card's expectation sentence.

A promise must be a quantity reality clears reliably: a low quantile. An order must cover the upside, because a stockout costs margin while an extra bottle of spirits costs a little capital: a high quantile. The mean is the one value that is wrong for both purposes simultaneously.

The fix — implementable this sprint

Split the field. promise_units = quantile(gap, 0.10) for the card's sentence and the promise-kept measurement; order_units = quantile(demand, q*) routed through the existing policy/inventory.py. Both quantiles come from the interval the forecast already returns.

Keep deficit_units as the ranking quantity — that is what the +16.6 point edge was measured on, and changing it would invalidate that result.

F3 · F12

The confidence on the card has never been checked against reality

critical · blocks every claim

The chain is built correctly and ends in nothing. deterministic.py sets confidence = p_hit, where p_hit = s/(1+s) and s = uplift × steadiness — a closed-form ratio. validator.py then calls _apply_calibration, which looks up an isotonic fit for the (family × demand_class) cell. That fit is produced by calibrate/fit.py from recorded outcomes. There are no recorded outcomes.

Evidence

calibrate/apply.py returns no_fit_for_cell:{family}/{demand_class} and passes the raw value through "WITH ITS REASON" — the module is explicit that an unexplained passthrough would become permanent. It is working as designed; it has nothing to work with.

So when a card says 74% likely, no one has ever verified that cards in that bucket succeed 74% of the time. The figure also multiplies into the ranking score, so an uncalibrated probability is reordering the slate.

The fix — one flag, then three weeks

Set REC_HOLDOUT_PCT = 20. Outcomes begin accumulating immediately; after roughly 50 per cell the isotonic fit becomes real and calibrate/monitor.py starts reporting Brier and ECE, both of which already exist.

Then make it a build gate: a card may not state a confidence whose reliability bin deviates by more than 10 points. Below that, state a band or state nothing.

F5

The service level is a business guess where it should be a derived number

critical · the profit lever

policy/inventory.py is the strongest module in the engine: σ comes from the p10–p90 interval, the divisor is computed from the quantile settings rather than hard-coded, and a forecast without a usable interval is refused. Its one weakness is the service level, which is a Setting per category family, PROVISIONAL, awaiting commercial sign-off.

It does not need sign-off. The service level is the critical fractile, and it is computable per SKU from figures already in the warehouse:

q* = Cu / (Cu + Co)

Cu  = unit margin            (price − cost)           already known where cost exists
Co  = carrying cost / cycle  = unit cost × WACC × days/365
                             + shelf opportunity
                             + obsolescence hazard   (≈ 0 for spirits, real for seasonal)

Spirits: $10 margin, ~$1 carrying   → q* ≈ 91%   — stock generously
Seasonal/limited: $10 margin, $4    → q* ≈ 71%   — stock tightly
The fix

Replace SERVICE_LEVEL_BY_FAMILY with a computed q*, keeping the existing Settings as the fallback when cost is absent — which is five of ten stores, so the fallback matters. Mark the computed path MEASURED and the fallback PROVISIONAL, so a card says which it used.

F6

The profitability verdict is measured against a broken ruler

critical · fix before anything else

"Four of five stores are net negative in April" is the headline that drove this whole investigation. The cost side of it is over_ordered_cost = over_units × unit_cost — the entire cost of goods, charged as though the stock were destroyed.

A case of bourbon that did not sell in April is not a loss. It is on the shelf in May. The real cost is capital at perhaps 10% a year plus shelf opportunity — on a $20 bottle held one extra month, cents, not $20. The current model overstates the downside by one to two orders of magnitude on exactly the products the engine recommends most.

The fix — do this first, it is a day's work

over_ordered_cost = over_units × unit_cost × (WACC/365 × days_held) + obsolescence_hazard × unit_cost, with the hazard near zero for spirits and real for dated or seasonal stock. Then re-run the five-store ledger.

Expect the sign to change at two or three stores. That is not good news to be celebrated — it is a measurement correction, and it must land before the sizing change so the sizing change is judged fairly.

F7

Zero sales and an empty shelf are the same row

high · the information ceiling

No module in the engine distinguishes "nobody wanted it" from "there was none to sell". The feature store correctly omits closed store days, but a SKU that was out of stock while the store traded contributes observed zeros to its own rate.

The damage runs in both directions, which is why it is worth fixing properly:

  • The rate is biased low, so r_long understates true demand, so the deficit is overstated — the engine promises more than the product can deliver. This is a direct contributor to the unkept promise.
  • The grade is corrupted. A SKU that was out of stock and gets restocked shows a large "lift" that the engine takes credit for predicting.
The fix — in two halves

Now, from existing data: infer a probable stockout as a day where a SKU with an established rhythm sold zero while its category traded normally, and treat those days as censored — excluded from the denominator rather than counted as zero demand. This is estimable today and testable against the few stores that do carry on-hand.

From tonight, properly: snapshot on-hand nightly per SKU. It cannot be backfilled, so every night without it is permanently lost evidence.

F8

The majority of SKUs get a method built for the minority

high · mis-specification

blend/shrink.py states it plainly: "intermittent SKUs are the majority, so this k is high". The engine knows the demand is intermittent and still models it as a rate with a shrunk mean. For a SKU selling in bursts of six every three weeks, a mean rate of 0.28 a day is arithmetically true and operationally meaningless.

There is a demand_class concept threaded through the calibration cells and the forecast is documented as using "the method the demand CLASS selects" — but there is no app/pipeline/forecast/ module, and no Croston, SBA or bootstrap implementation anywhere. The class currently selects between variations of one method.

The fix

Classify properly (Syntetos–Boylan on average inter-demand interval and the squared coefficient of variation), then pick the method by class: the shrunk mean for smooth, Croston/SBA for intermittent, and an empirical bootstrap over historical windows for lumpy — which also produces an honest interval instead of a fitted band, and so partly subsumes the three-attempt interval workaround.

Pure standard library throughout, which a bootstrap is and a gradient-boosted model is not.

Where 90% actually arrives, phase by phase

After P1 — the 90% promise. "At least N more units" kept 85–95% of the time, verifiable by the owner against their own till. This is the guarantee to put on the card, and it is reachable with no new data.

After P2 — the 90% confidence. A card that says 90% belongs to a bucket that succeeds 90% of the time, with a reliability diagram to show it.

After P4 — the 90% range. Interval coverage at p5–p95 ≥ 90% across all four horizons, for the right reason rather than by widening the band until it cannot be wrong.

After P5 — the 90% that pays. Of the cards the engine chooses to issue, 90% make money. Coverage falls and is reported honestly; "nothing worth doing today" becomes a legitimate answer instead of a slate filled to a quota.

The one thing not to do

Do not raise the p_hit floor to manufacture the number

It is the obvious move and the engine's own settings already price it: 0.50 gives 75.9% at 5.1 cards a run; 0.55 buys about one point, cuts coverage to 2.4 cards and halves the margin. A floor high enough to show 90% would issue almost nothing.

A dashboard reading 90% beside an empty slate is a worse product than one reading 76% with five useful cards, and the owner will notice within a week. Abstention in P5 is a different thing entirely: it withholds cards whose expected profit is negative, and it publishes the coverage it chose.

Section 06

What no model can fix

The selection ceiling at roughly 73% is an information limit. Three fields would move it, none of them can be backfilled, and every night they are not captured is evidence permanently lost. This is the part of the programme that is not engineering.

CaptureWhy it breaks the ceilingCost of waiting
Nightly on-hand per SKU Separates "nobody wanted it" from "the shelf was empty" — the single confusion that caps selection accuracy. Also makes a reorder point computable rather than assumed Unrecoverable. Every night without it is a night that can never be used for training or grading
Received / delivered dates Lead time becomes measured instead of declared, per vendor. Every reorder date on every card currently rests on an assumption purchase_orders has the columns and zero rows at four of five stores. One conversation per store
Price-change events A dip caused by a competitor's promotion is not a dip to recover from. Realised unit price is already stored; the event is not Partly reconstructable from realised prices, which is a weaker signal than the event itself
The holdout arm Turns forecasting evidence into causal evidence, and is the only thing that can ever answer "does acting on this advice cause the sale?" It is a flag. Zero engineering cost, and it has been off for the entire programme
And a sentence to keep saying out loud

Nobody has acted on a single recommendation in any measurement produced so far. Every figure in every report, including the +16.6 point edge, says the engine points at products that go on to move. None of it says that acting on the advice causes the movement. Those are different claims and only the holdout separates them — which is why P0 turns it on before anything else is built on top.

How this was produced. Every finding was verified by reading the source on branch Enh_Engine_v1, not from documentation: the sizing path in app/recommender/deterministic.py, the calibration chain through app/pipeline/calibrate/apply.py and fit.py, the unused service-level machinery in app/pipeline/policy/inventory.py, the absence of any newsvendor, censoring, cannibalisation or budget logic, and the measured precision/coverage prices recorded in the MIN_HIT_PROBABILITY setting. The distributional figures (53.5%, 89.1%, 93.8%, q* = 90.9%) are Poisson computations for a mean of 14 units over 30 days — the engine's own median pick size — and are reproducible in a few lines of standard library.

What is judgement rather than measurement. The phase durations are estimates. The claim that correcting the carrying-cost model will change the sign at two or three stores is a prediction, not a result — it has to be re-run to know. The ~73% selection ceiling is an empirical finding from three measurements on five stores, not a theorem; new information could move it, which is exactly what section 06 proposes. Everything else on this page is either a line of code or an arithmetic identity.
