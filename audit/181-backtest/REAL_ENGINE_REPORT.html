1. Two nulls, and why the second one matters

The engine selects SKUs whose recent window is depressed against their own long run. The metric asks whether they then beat that recent window. Those are close to the same question, so part of any edge is regression to the mean rather than foresight. A report that did not say so would be flattering itself.

So two baselines are computed. The volume-matched null is the usual one: random baskets of similar-sized products. The reversion-matched null is the hard one — random baskets drawn from SKUs that were equally primed to revert. The engine's edge over that is the part that is not mechanical, and it is the number to quote to a sceptic.

Which significance test, and why. Each decision run shows about five cards, so a permutation test per run is nearly powerless — five coin flips cannot be significant. Combining 24 such tests by Fisher's method returns p = 0.73, which is a test discarding its own evidence, not a null result. The figure above pools every run's picks and compares them against pooled matched-random slates drawn run by run, so clustering is honoured and the power is not thrown away. Both are printed by the script; neither is hidden.

2c. The spread, not just the median

A median lift of 1.52× could come from many small wins or a few enormous ones. Red bars are picks that did not beat their own rate — they are part of the record.

2d. Does its confidence mean anything?

A confidence that does not track outcomes is worse than none, because it both ranks the cards and gets printed on them. Sheet 08 measured the previous engine at 0.48 stated against 0.23 actual; this is the same check on the current one.

This one is not a clean result, and pretending otherwise would be the easiest lie in the report. The hit rate rises across the confidence range, but not monotonically — the fourth fifth outscores the fifth. So p_hit is doing its job as a gate (everything above the floor beats 70%) and doing it poorly as a fine-grained ranking within the eligible set.

The practical consequence: showing the top 3 cards instead of the top 10 is not reliably better, because the ordering inside the slate carries little information. The honest lever is the floor, not the rank. Sharpening this is the clearest next piece of work on the engine.

Read the PER CARD column, not the total. The two arms do not place the same number of bets — 1704 cards against 2520 — because sheet 20 shows only what clears its bar. Comparing raw totals across different card counts compares a rate to a volume and calls the difference a result.

And be honest about coverage. Sheet 20 touches $195,583 of margin flow where the old ordering touched $1,621,180. It is a smaller, sharper intervention: fewer cards, smaller products, far higher accuracy. If the goal is to influence as much of the shop as possible, that is a real trade-off and the confidence floor is the dial — lowering it to 0.40 restores a full ten-card slate at about four points of hit rate.

Two margin numbers, and only one of them is a verdict. total captured is sold × margin — dominated by how big the picks are, so an engine that simply names the shop's biggest sellers maximises it while predicting nothing. incremental is (sold − expected) × margin — money that would not have arrived anyway. Judge a recommendation on the second.

5b. Data quality — what this store's feed got wrong

4 line item(s) were refused before any figure on this page was computed. A single line cannot plausibly be more than 1000 units — across the pilot stores the median line is 1 unit and the 99th percentile is 20.

This matters beyond tidiness. Before the contract was applied, the largest of these alone reported an incremental margin of $98.6M on a store turning over $7M a year. A number that wrong is easy to catch; the danger is the same defect one order of magnitude smaller, which would look plausible and quietly move every rate for that product.

This is a POS data-entry problem, not an engine problem — but it is the engine's job to refuse it, and worth raising with whoever owns the till.

6. What this does not prove

This measures forecasting, not causation. Nobody acted on these recommendations — they were generated retrospectively. It shows the engine can point at products that were about to move. It does not show that following the advice causes extra sales. Only a live holdout, where some recommendations are shown and others deliberately withheld, proves that. Production has the mechanism (REC_HOLDOUT_PCT, FNV-1a keyed on client:location:sku:family); it needs switching on.
  • One store. Every figure is Cork Runner Wine & Spirits. Another store with different categories or a different customer base could look materially different - which is exactly why this report is produced per store rather than pooled.
  • 1704 measurable predictions. Enough to detect a large effect, not enough to measure a small one precisely — which is why every number carries an interval, and why the headline interval is clustered by date rather than by row.
  • No live inventory. The production engine also sees stock levels, which drive its understock and overstock recommendations. This replay had no historical on-hand, so those families are absent and the engine is being judged on less than it normally has.
  • 90% is not reachable on this metric. For a SKU selling ~10 units a month, Poisson noise alone puts it below its own mean about a third of the time regardless of what is known in advance.

7. Architecture

No model chooses anything. The LLM writes the sentence after the decision is fixed, so a model outage costs fluency and never a recommendation. Python holds no analytics credentials and opens no database connection — the whole pipeline is a pure function of one payload, which is what makes a card reproducible months later.
