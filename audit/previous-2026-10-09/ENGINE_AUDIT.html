Does the recommendation engine work,
store by store?

Three layers of evidence for each store: every card issued in April 2026 and what it did, how wrong the forecast is against three baselines, and a walk-forward backtest across 2.3 years.

5 stores · generated 08 October 2026 · every figure produced by app/pipeline, the production modules, called directly

Per store, never pooled

The engine is switched on one store at a time, so it is judged one store at a time. A pooled average would also let the largest store carry the result — Cork Runner has four times Bethany’s line items, so a units-weighted mean is mostly one store wearing five hats.

Read the edge column, not the hit rate. A hit rate of 72% sounds like skill until you learn that products which have just dipped beat their own recent rate about 59% of the time anyway. The edge over a reversion-matched null is what is left after that.

The promise is calibrated at 1 of 5 stores, and that is the open problem

A hit only says a product beat its own recent rate. It says nothing about the quantity the card promised — and a card saying “expect 40 more” when 12 arrive has not been useful, however the hit is scored. Measured per store:

Mango Jo's Bar & Liquors 98% · Marshfield Wine & Spirits 83% · Northlake Liquors North 60% · Cork Runner Wine & Spirits 50% · Bethany Discount Liquor 19%

A per-store scaling factor, fitted on each store’s own closed windows, was validated out-of-sample at Bethany — 50% to 93% on dates it never saw. It does not generalise. Only 1 store(s) land in a defensible band, and the conclusion is that one scalar per store is not enough: where the reversion relationship shifts between the calibration period and the measured month, the factor is stale.

Marshfield shows the fallback working as designed — it has history only from February 2025, so there are not enough closed windows to fit on and every card uses the identity. That is the right behaviour (do not invent a correction) and it is why its promise is the worst of the five.

Consequence: 0 of 5 stores with cost data are net negative in April once the stock the cards called for and did not sell is costed. The engine is right about DIRECTION almost everywhere and wrong about SIZE almost everywhere, and no hit rate can show that.

2The gate, check by check

Both gates must hold for a store to ship. A gate that does not apply does not block — a store sending no unit cost cannot be judged on money — but a check that was not measured is never a pass. Every threshold here is a claim somebody can say is wrong, in which case it gets changed deliberately rather than drifting.

One check was retired, and this is why

promise_kept asked for 85–95% of cards to deliver at least the promised increment. That is unreachable at any store under any promise, by one line of arithmetic: a promise of at least one unit can only be kept on a card whose realised increment is at least one unit, so promise_kept ≤ hit rate — and the hit rates are 83 / 76 / 71 / 62 / 49%. It was the accuracy target restated as an independent test, and it could not pass until the thing it was meant to verify already had. The field wired into it was a ratio of totals rather than a per-card rate, so the number being compared to the band was not even the same kind of quantity.

It is replaced by promise_unbiased, which asks the separable question it had buried: is the stated quantity systematically too big or too small? The kept rate is still measured and printed, because the reason it is not gated has to stay checkable.

3What this does not prove

The confidence curve does not reach production yet

The calibration_ece and no_unreliable_confidence checks above are measured honestly — the curve is fitted on January and February and scored on March, so no store is graded on a slice it was fitted to. But the fitted curve is not wired into the live card path. to_raw_recommendations still ships the raw p_hit, and it says so in as many words: the number orders cards against each other and is deliberately not presented to an owner as a percentage.

So these two checks say the calibration method works on this store's data. They do not say an owner is currently seeing a calibrated percentage, because no owner is seeing a percentage at all. Closing that gap needs the fitted bundle persisted and loaded on the live route, which in turn needs scored outcomes in recommendation_evaluations — and whether that table is actually populated in the deployed environment is still unverified. Until then a passing ECE is a statement about the engine's capacity, not about today's cards.

Forecasting, not causation

Nobody acted on any of these recommendations. They were generated retrospectively from data the engine could legitimately have seen each morning, so this shows the engine points at products that go on to move. It does not show that acting on the advice causes the movement. Only a live holdout — some cards shown, others deliberately withheld — proves that, and production has the mechanism but it is not switched on.

  • No shelf counts anywhere. Historical on-hand does not exist in the warehouse, so a dip caused by a stockout is indistinguishable from a dip caused by slow demand. That contaminates the measurement in both directions, and it is the single thing most likely to change these numbers once captured.
  • Lead times are declared, not measured. Nothing records when an order actually arrived, so every order quantity rests on an assumption.
  • One month per store for the ledger. The backtest spans 2.3 years and is the quotable figure; the month is the one worth checking line by line.
