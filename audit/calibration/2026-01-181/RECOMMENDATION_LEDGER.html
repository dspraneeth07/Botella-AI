Did it make money in April?

The cards promised 2,061 extra units and 1,805 arrived — 88% of the promise. After costing the units they called for that did not sell, April is $+1,129. An earlier version of this engine promised about twice what arrived and was net negative here; the deficit is now calibrated against this store’s own closed windows.

6Did it keep its promise?

A hit only says a product beat its own recent rate. It says nothing about the quantity the card promised — and a card that says “expect 40 more” when 12 arrive has not been useful, however the hit is scored. This is the measure that matters most, and the one the engine used to fail.

Above 100% means the card understated: more arrived than it claimed, so an owner who ordered to the card ran short. That is a different error from over-promising, not a better outcome.

11What this does not prove

Forecasting, not causation

Nobody acted on these recommendations — they were generated retrospectively from data the engine could legitimately have seen each morning. So this shows the engine pointed at products that went on to move. It does not show that acting on the advice caused the movement. Only a live holdout, where some cards are shown and others deliberately withheld, proves that.

  • Overlapping windows. Cards issued on consecutive days largely watch the same future, so the 139 cards are not 139 independent experiments. The confidence intervals above treat them as independent and are therefore optimistic; the fortnightly backtest exists to avoid this and is the better source for a headline number.
  • One month, one store. March is not every month, and a quieter or busier season would move these figures.
  • No shelf counts. The engine never saw stock levels, so no understock or overstock card appears here, and a dip caused by a stockout is indistinguishable from a dip caused by slow demand.
