2Why this store’s numbers moved

1 — The money moved because the ruler was wrong, not because the picks changed

This store read $+791 and now reads $+791. The old figure charged the entire cost of goods for every unit a card said to buy that did not sell, as though the stock had been destroyed. Liquor does not spoil: a surplus bottle is deferred revenue, and its real cost is capital tied up, shelf space and the chance it never sells at full price — cents a month on a $20 bottle, not $20. On the old ruler the same month reads $-601, so the correction here is worth $+1,392.

The engine picked the same products. Nothing in this row should be read as the engine having improved.

2 — The card set did not change

98 cards before and after, and the 30-day hit rate is 56.1% either way. This store had no lottery or cigarette lines among its recommendations, so the merchandise screen had nothing to remove here. Its money still moved, for the reason above.

3 — The promise column changed its meaning, not just its value

It read — and now reads 18%, and it was labelled promise kept. It never was a kept rate: it is total units delivered over total units promised, so 100% means the promise carried no systematic bias, below means over-promising and above means under-promising. For comparison, the fraction of individual cards that met their promise is 30.6% — a different statistic, bounded above by the hit rate, and the one the retired check was demanding 85–95% of.

The old gate banded this 85–95%, which excluded an unbiased promise and demanded a permanent 5–15% shortfall. It is now banded 85–115% and renamed promise_unbiased.

4 — The stated confidence is now a measured probability — and it fails

The raw p_hit is a ranking score, not a probability, and the card says so in as many words. Fitting the isotonic curve on 265 cards from January and February and scoring 98 cards in March — so this store is not graded on a slice it was fitted to — gives an expected calibration error of 9.7 points against a bar of 5. That is a real failure and it is published.

A reliability bin needs 30 outcomes to count: at n=30 and p=0.5 the binomial standard error is 9.1 points, which is the floor at which a 10-point per-bin test stops measuring noise. A five-card bin was condemning a whole store before this.

5 — The card now publishes a range it can be held to

Every card used to state a point — “about 78 units” — which invites “you said 78 and I got 61, you were wrong”. It now also states a two-sided 90% range sized to that product’s own measured scatter, and that range is what the interval_coverage check tests. Measuring the coverage of an interval the owner never sees would be scoring the engine on a claim it never made.

4What these numbers do not prove

The confidence curve does not reach production

The calibration checks above are measured out of sample, but the fitted curve is not wired into the live card path. to_raw_recommendations still ships the raw p_hit. So they say the method works on this store’s data; they do not say an owner is seeing a calibrated percentage, because no owner is seeing a percentage at all.

  • Nobody acted on any of these recommendations. They were generated retrospectively from data the engine could legitimately have seen each morning. This shows the engine points at products that go on to move; it does not show that acting on the advice causes the movement.
  • No shelf counts. Historical on-hand does not exist in the warehouse, so a dip caused by a stockout is indistinguishable from a dip caused by slow demand.
  • Lead times are declared, not measured. Nothing records when an order actually arrived, so every order quantity rests on an assumption.
  • 98 cards across 21 products. Overlapping cards on the same product are treated as independent, so a narrow catalogue flatters these rates.
