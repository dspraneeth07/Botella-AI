Engine Three · architecture change record · branch Enh_Engine_v1

The criterion was the defect

What actually changed in the architecture since the component specification — measured, with the eight ideas that measurement threw out and the one problem it exposed and did not solve. Four new stages, one rewritten ranking basis, nothing else moved.

Date 4 October 2026 Companion to the thirteen-component specification of 22 September State 1,265 tests · both build guards clean · 120 declared settings

One line changed the engine, and it was not a model

The specification got the layers right and the ranking basis wrong. Expected value was multiplied by total demand, estimated from a trailing 28-day window. Ranking on that quantity orders products by how big a seller each one looks right now, so it selects the windows that are most inflated — and an inflated window is by definition the one most likely to fall back. The selection criterion and the estimation error were the same number.

Multiplying instead by the deficit — the units the current plan misses — took the pooled hit rate from 31.0% to 75.9% and incremental margin per card from −$952 to +$48. Against a null that already knows about mean reversion the edge is +16.6 points at p = 0.0005, and it holds at all five pilot stores (+6.1 to +19.9). No new data, no new model, no new field from the POS.

What is not fixed, stated here rather than in a footnote: the engine is right about direction almost everywhere and wrong about size almost everywhere. Four of five stores are net negative in the month the cards were written for, once the stock they called for and did not sell is costed. A per-store calibration fixed that at one store and did not generalise to the other four.

Section 01

The change itself

The specification shrank each estimate toward a peer cohort — across stores. The new stage shrinks a SKU's recent rate toward its own long run — across time. Same arithmetic, second axis:

sheet 06, already built: shrink this store toward stores like it.
sheet 20, the new stage:  shrink this window toward this SKU's own history.

        rate_hat = ( n_eff · r_recent  +  k · r_long ) / ( n_eff + k )
        n_eff    = open_days / ( 1 + cv² )

  n_eff is the textbook overdispersion deflation — an erratic four weeks
  carries less information than a steady four weeks, so it is worth less.

  k is NOT a new constant. It is the registry's existing
  shrink.k.sku_velocity, so the two axes cannot drift apart.

and then the part that actually changed the ranking:

  BEFORE   score = margin × rate_hat × horizon        # the LEVEL
  AFTER    score = margin × (rate_hat − r_recent) × horizon   # the GAP

  open days throughout. never calendar days.

The difference is not a refinement of the same ordering — it reverses it. A product whose window is running hot has a large level and a negative gap; it used to rank first and is now refused outright. That is the whole mechanism:

Why this is structure and not a fit

The hit rate is flat at 68.0 / 68.0 / 68.7 / 68.4 / 68.0 for k = 8 / 13 / 21 / 30 / 45. Insensitivity across a five-fold range of the only free parameter is the signature of a real effect; a tuned one collapses when you move the knob.

A version that scored higher is deliberately not in the code. A rank-average of the same two signals hit 74.8% — then fell to 64.9% and 50.4% as a materiality floor was applied, because its edge was borrowed from products selling about one unit a month, where the ratio is noise amplified by division. The shippable version scores lower and keeps scoring it.

The second output, and the rule attached to it

The stage returns two numbers, and confusing them is how a card ends up carrying a fabricated dollar figure. The separation is enforced at the type level, not by convention:

OutputWhat it isWhere it is allowed to goMay be multiplied by margin?
deficit_unitsUnits the current plan missesMoney — units_at_stake in the scoreYes — it is a unit count
p_hitLikelihood it beats its baselineConfidence — sheet 08 calibrationNever — it is a ratio

And p_hit itself was rebuilt. It originally averaged a squashed uplift with steadiness, which lets either term carry a candidate alone — so a perfectly steady seller had steadiness = 1.0 whether it was climbing or dying, and cv = 0 forced p_hit ≥ 0.5 however hard the product was falling:

  BEFORE   p_hit = mean( squash(uplift), steadiness )   # either term can carry it
  AFTER    p_hit = s / (1 + s),   s = uplift × steadiness   # both must hold

  chosen on the first 25 decision dates, scored on the last 25 it never saw:
  68.8% → 69.6%. it scored BETTER out of sample. structure, not a fit.

  and the floor now reads as a sentence: p_hit ≥ 0.5 is exactly
  uplift × steadiness ≥ 1 — the long run must exceed the recent rate by (1 + cv).

Even with the product form, a steady decliner cleared the confidence floor, so recommendability requires three things rather than one. The third is what refuses product A above:

def is_recommendable(reverted, ctx, config) -> bool:
    return (reverted.material                       # ≥ MATERIALITY_FLOOR_UNITS
            and reverted.deficit_units > 0.0        # a negative gap is NOT a small one
            and reverted.p_hit >= config.get(MIN_HIT_PROBABILITY, ctx))
Section 02

Where the four new stages sit

The five layers are unchanged and so are nine of the thirteen components. Four stages were added and one was rewritten in place. The thing to read in the diagram is the order inside layer 2: the new stage runs before the cohort blend, so the quantity shrunk across stores is one that has already been shrunk across time.

What is wired into the live route, and what is not

Being exact about this matters more than the diagram. The deficit gate is live: app/recommender/deterministic.py imports revert and is_recommendable and screens on them, stamping versions={"temporal_reversion": "1.1.0"} on every run. The calendar facts are wired into app/pipeline/features/build.py.

The lifecycle memory and the full merchandise contract are not yet on the route. They are built, tested and exercised by the audit harness; the live path still uses its own narrower is_merchandise screen and has no card memory between mornings. Wiring them in is the next migration step, not a finished one, and no number on this page depends on pretending otherwise.

S15

Merchandise contract — and it nearly shipped broken

M8 · the most useful page in this record

The defect. 0123456789 at 3,506 units a month, SKU 12 at ten cents (a bottle deposit), and bags of ice reached the top of the earnings table. Huge volume, a wobbling trailing window — exactly the shape the reversion signal hunts. The engine was working correctly on data that should never have reached it.

The near-miss. The first version of the filter was audited against all ten stores' catalogues before shipping, and would have deleted real stock:

Rule, as first writtenWhat it would have refused

Rewritten to exact forms and phrases, with word-boundary matching for single words, and the price floor demoted from a refusal to a flag: 45 of 44,398 SKUs refused — 0.10%, every one accountable.

The sentence that is now in the module

A filter that removes real stock is worse than the mess it cleans up. It is in the source, beside a REMOVED_TERMS constant that documents what was dropped and why, so the broad version cannot come back as a well-meaning simplification.

A quiet bug this class produces

The word-boundary rule depends on a literal \b reaching the regex. Written through a shell heredoc it became \x08, a backspace character — which matches nothing and silently disabled the entire word filter while every test that only checked exact forms still passed. The module is built with explicit escaping for that reason.

S17

Calendar facts — kept. The candidate they powered — rejected

built, measured, half discarded

Moving dates are computed, never tabulated: Thanksgiving is the fourth Thursday, Memorial Day the last Monday, the Super Bowl moved to the second Sunday in February in 2022, and Easter needs the anonymous Gregorian computus. 22 tests pin known-good dates across four years so the arithmetic cannot drift silently, and a run in 2031 needs no edit.

Then the honest part. A seasonal multiplier built from those facts — with the day-of-week and pre-holiday indices estimated from each store's own history rather than hand-written — lost at 0 of 4 horizons, pushing 30-day bias from +0.6% to +9.2%.

The trailing window already reflects current conditions, so a store-level busy-ness index double-counts. This is the second time seasonality has been tried on this engine and lost. The harness prints REJECT with the reason rather than quietly omitting the arm.

What survives, and why

The calendar facts stay, because they are inputs a model can learn a coefficient for. The multiplier does not, because it asserted one.

Section 04

What the measurement threw out

A record that lists only what worked is not evidence. Eight ideas were built far enough to measure and then removed — including two that scored better than what shipped.

The prediction interval took three attempts, and only the third is honest

AttemptCoverage, target 75–85%Why it failed
Weekly cv applied to the window total91–93%So wide it could not be wrong — a band that is always right measures nothing
cv / √weeks37–62%Too narrow. Weekly demand is autocorrelated, so the √n deflation does not apply

Four statistical choices that change the headline

  • A reversion-matched null, not a volume-matched one. The engine selects products whose window is depressed, and the metric asks whether they beat that window — close to the same question, so part of any edge is regression to the mean. Against a volume-matched null the edge reads +44 points; against products equally primed to revert it is +16.6. The second one is the one quoted.
  • A date-clustered bootstrap. Two picks on the same morning share a market. A row-wise bootstrap reported "82.9%" off 41 observations and was thrown away.
  • A pooled permutation test, not per-run. Each run shows about five cards, so a per-run test is nearly powerless — Fisher over 24 of them returned p = 0.73 while the clustered interval comfortably excluded the null. Pooling across runs while drawing the null run-by-run gives p = 0.0005. Both are printed, because the discrepancy is informative rather than embarrassing.
  • Incremental margin, not total. (sold − expected) × margin, not sold × margin. The old ordering showed the largest total captured, $215k, and a negative incremental — total margin rewards naming the biggest sellers while predicting nothing about them.
Section 05

What this does not fix

Two claims from the earlier work are now known to be wrong, and both were mine. They are here rather than in a footnote because an architecture record that hides its retractions cannot be used to make the next decision.

Retraction 1 — I validated at one store and generalised from it

The promised quantity, not the direction, is the open problem. A per-store scaling factor fitted on each store's own closed windows took Bethany from 50% to 93% of its promise kept, out of sample, on dates the factor never saw. I reported the promise fixed. Across five stores it holds at one.

Every store beats a reversion-matched null significantly. That is the result that holds, and it is what "ship" means in the audit index. Four of five are net negative in April. That is the result that does not, and "ship" does not mean "makes money".

Marshfield shows the fallback working as designed: it has history only from February 2025, so there are too few closed windows to fit a factor on, and every one of its 169 cards uses the identity. Refusing to invent a correction is the right behaviour, and it is why its promise is the worst of the five. One scalar per store is not enough — where the reversion relationship shifts between the calibration period and the measured month, the factor is stale. The most honest next step is probably to promise a range rather than a point.

Retraction 2 — 90% was not reachable, and asking for it was the wrong question

Measured three independent ways. The top decile of the strongest available signal caps at 65–69% at every horizon. Sweeping floor × coverage over 50 decision dates, the best worst-horizon result is 73.1%. Taking only the single best card per date gives 71.1%.

The reason is arithmetic, not engineering: for a product selling about ten units a month, Poisson noise alone puts it below its own mean roughly a third of the time, whatever is known about it. A 90% hit rate on this metric would be evidence of a leak, not of skill. The edge over the reversion-matched null is the number that can rise.

Why 106 of 120 settings still say PROVISIONAL on every CI run

The registry prints the status of each declared setting at build time, and a PROVISIONAL value is one that was chosen by judgement rather than by a sweep. Leaving that count loud is deliberate: it is the difference between an engine with five measured parameters and 115 honest guesses, and an engine that pretends all 120 were earned.

What changed from the specification, in one paragraph. The five layers, the thirteen components, the shrinkage continuum, the deterministic composer, the writer-only model and the gate all carry over. Four stages were inserted — temporal reversion in layer 2, the merchandise contract and the card lifecycle in layer 3, computed calendar facts in layer 1 — and one quantity was swapped inside the composer: expected value is now multiplied by the deficit rather than by total demand. That swap is the measured gain. The seasonal multiplier those calendar facts were built to power was rejected by its own measurement and is not in the code.

Grounding. Every figure on this page is produced by the modules in app/pipeline called directly — not by a port or a reimplementation — over exports of the five pilot stores taken from syncgo with read-only queries. Outcomes are graded by app/pipeline/outcomes/lift.py, the single lift definition pinned digit-for-digit against backend/lib/lift.js. The backtest spans 52 decision dates across 2.3 years at fortnightly spacing; the ledger covers March 2026 followed 30, 60, 90 and 120 days out. One claim on this page is not a measurement: that wiring the lifecycle and merchandise modules into the live route is the next step is a judgement, and the migration has not been done.
