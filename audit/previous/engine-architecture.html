The engine was ranking on the quantity it was getting wrong

Every enhancement on this branch, the mechanism each one changed, and the measurement that justified it — including the eight ideas that their own measurement killed.

1,265 tests · both build guards clean · 120 declared settings, 106 still provisional
measured across 5 pilot stores · 3.3M transaction lines · 610 cards examined card by card

1 · The defect, and the one-line fix

A demand rate estimated from a trailing 28-day window is noisy: sometimes it catches a quiet spell, sometimes a spike. Ranking candidates by that rate orders products by how big a seller they look right now — so it selects the ones whose windows are most inflated, and an inflated window is by definition the one most likely to fall back.

The selection criterion and the estimation error were the same number.

Against a volume-matched null — a random pick of similar-sized products — the edge reads +44 points. That baseline is too easy: part of any edge over it is regression to the mean, which anyone picking quiet products gets for free. +16.6 is the number to quote.

2 · Where the four new stages sit

Nothing was rewritten. Four stages were added to a pipeline that already had sixteen, and each sits at the point where its evidence exists.

3 · The engine had no memory

Stroyski 1.75L was recommended on eight consecutive trading days and missed every one. The engine recomputed the same depressed window each morning with no idea it had already said this, or that the prediction was visibly failing.

Measured

March 2026 at Bethany: 162 cards became 69, at the same hit rate. Stroyski pulled after three days instead of eight. Seventeen retirements — thirteen declining, one no-recovery, three stale.

Every store beats a reversion-matched null, significantly

+6.1 to +19.9 points, all at p = 0.0005. The engine has real skill beyond mean reversion at every store tested, and that result holds.

Four of five are net negative in April

A hit only says a product beat its own recent rate. It says nothing about the quantity the card promised — and a card saying "expect 40 more" when 12 arrive has not been useful, however the hit is scored.

A per-store scaling factor fitted on each store's own closed windows was validated out of sample at Bethany: 50% to 93% on dates it never saw. It does not generalise. The promise is kept at one store of five. Where the reversion relationship shifts between the calibration period and the measured month, the factor is stale.

Marshfield shows the fallback working as designed: it has history only from February 2025, so there are too few closed windows to fit on and every card uses the identity factor. Refusing to invent a correction is right, and it is why its promise is the worst of the five.

The engine is right about direction almost everywhere and wrong about size almost everywhere. No hit rate can show that. It is the open problem.

The merchandise filter nearly deleted real stock

Deposits, lottery lines and ice bags reached the top of the earnings table, because a mechanic rung on every transaction has huge volume and a wobbling trailing window — exactly the shape the reversion signal hunts. But the first version of the filter, audited against all ten stores' catalogues before shipping, would have refused 1690 Absolut Mandrin (an "ascending digits" pattern that matches almost any number), 417 E&J Brandy and 105 Keg Miller Lite (a four-character SKU minimum), Department 66 Grenache — a winery — and LYNX POLARIZED GROUNDING AD., caught because it contains "rounding".

Rewritten to exact forms and phrases with word-boundary matching: 45 of 44,398 SKUs refused, every one accountable. A filter that removes real stock is worse than the mess it cleans up.

7 · What is blocked, and why

  • Historical on-hand does not exist. A stockout is indistinguishable from a dip, so the engine recommends the product and scores the restock as skill — noise in the selection and in the grade. It cannot be backfilled; capture has to start now, and it is the single thing most likely to move these numbers.
  • Lead times are declared, never measured. purchase_orders has the right columns and zero rows at four of five stores. Nothing records when an order arrived, so every order quantity rests on an assumption.
  • No cross-store model. app/pipeline is pure stdlib by design — no numpy, scipy or LightGBM. A global model trains offline and the pipeline consumes a fitted artefact; the boundary stays a file.
  • Causation is unproven. recommendation_evaluations has zero rows. The holdout mechanism exists and is not switched on, so nothing here shows that acting on a card causes the sale.

8 · On the 90% target

Measured three ways. The top decile of the strongest available signal caps at 65–69% at every horizon. Sweeping the confidence floor against coverage over fifty decision dates, the best worst-horizon result is 73.1%. The best single card per date is 71.1%.

90% is not reachable on this metric. For a product selling about ten units a month, Poisson noise alone puts it below its own mean roughly a third of the time regardless of what is known in advance. The reversion-matched null sits at 59%, so today's 76% is already +16.6 points of real skill — closer to the ceiling than the gap to 90% suggests.

The gap worth closing first is not 76% → 90%. It is a promise kept at one store in five.
